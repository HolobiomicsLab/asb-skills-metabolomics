# mass-spectral-data-post-processing

## Summary

Post-process LC-MS metabolomics datasets with the R-based MDRB (mass dataset relevance bridging) technique, which scores metabolite-prototype pairs by sequentially integrating m/z, MS1/MS2 mass defect, and MS2 fragment similarity to bridge prototypes and metabolites of TCM samples.

## When to use

Use when you have unlabeled MS1/MS2 features from herbal or TCM plasma/tissue samples and need to bridge in vivo metabolites back to prototype components without predicting structures beforehand.

## When NOT to use

- Input is already a curated, annotated metabolite list with established prototype-metabolite assignments — no bridging is required
- The MS acquisition lacks MS2 fragment data, since MDRB requires the similarity of MS2 fragments as one of its three scoring dimensions
- The goal is targeted quantification of known compounds rather than non-targeted identification and bridging of unknown metabolites

## Inputs

- Prototype component database (in vitro component list with structures and MS2 fragments)
- Unpredictable-metabolite feature table produced by PATBS from LC-MS runs (positive and negative ion modes)
- AUC-pooled plasma peak-area table with feature-level peak areas per ion mode

## Outputs

- mass_defect_optimization.csv (per-range pathway coverage, positivity rate, false positives, and selected ±25 mmu optimum)
- cyanogenic_glycoside_relative_abundance.csv (ion mode, feature IDs, summed peak area, relative abundance percentage)
- Component relationship (bridging) network linking in vivo metabolites to prototypes sharing the same parent nucleus

## How to apply

Load a prototype database and the unpredictable-metabolite feature table (generated by PATBS background subtraction) into the R-based MDRB platform from github.com/933ZhangDD/MDRB. Score metabolite-prototype pairs by sequentially integrating the three dimensions of mass spectral data — m/z, mass defect of MS1 and MS2, and similarity of MS2 fragments — using structural similarity and minimal mass defect variations. Optimize the MS1/MS2 mass defect range by running the match at ±10, ±25, ±50, and ±75 mmu and tabulating pathway coverage, positivity rate, and false-positive bridged pairs per range; ±25 mmu was the reported optimum because narrower ranges lose metabolic pathway coverage while wider ranges raise false positives. Build the component relationship network from the bridged pairs and quantify exposure as feature peak-area shares per ion mode (e.g., cyanogenic glycosides contributed 43.16% positive / 53.57% negative mode of pooled plasma peak area in the CKXR study). Write per-range metrics and relative-abundance results to structured CSV outputs for validation against reported values.

## Evaluation signals

- Mass-defect range optimization reproduces the ±10/±25/±50/±75 mmu comparison (Fig. S3) and selects ±25 mmu as the balance of pathway coverage, positivity rate, and false positives
- Recomputed cyanogenic glycoside peak-area shares match 43.16% (positive ion mode) and 53.57% (negative ion mode) within ±0.5 percentage points
- Every bridged pair is supported by all three scoring dimensions (m/z, MS1/MS2 mass defect, MS2 fragment similarity), with mass defects of MS2 fragments of prototypes and metabolites within the configured range
- Output CSVs contain the required columns (ion mode, feature IDs, summed peak area, relative abundance percentage) with no missing prototype assignments
- The component relationship network connects in vivo and in vitro components sharing the same parent nucleus structure

## Limitations

- No software versions are stated for the MDRB technique or its data-processing platform, complicating exact reproducibility
- No statistical validation, replicate counts, or quantitative performance metrics are reported for the strategy
- No specific mass spectrometry instrument model or data acquisition parameters are named, limiting transferability
- Identification of in vivo components remains difficult despite MS data post-processing techniques
- Predecessor approaches such as MTSF only enable single metabolite-to-database matching using only MS2 similarity, which MDRB addresses but predictable responses rely on Metworks-provided rules

## Evidence

- [abstract] Sequential three-dimension scoring underpins the MDRB workflow: "The current method integrates three dimensions of mass spectral data including m/z, mass defect of MS1 and MS2, and the similarity of MS2 fragments, which was sequentially analyzed"
- [abstract] Scoring rationale based on structural similarity and minimal mass defect: "We developed a scoring approach based on the structural similarity and minimal mass defect variations between metabolites and prototypes."
- [intro] Origin of the MDRB post-processing technique described in the introduction: "we integrated the mass spectrometry data of m/z, MS2 fragment matching and mass defect of the difference limit, to establish a novel mass dataset relevance bridging (MDRB)"
- [intro] Mass defect range of MS2 fragments provides the bridging rationale: "the mass defect of MS2 fragments of prototypes and its metabolites is within a certain range, which can likewise provide critical information for bridging"
