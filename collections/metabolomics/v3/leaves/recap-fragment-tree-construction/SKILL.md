# recap-fragment-tree-construction

## Summary

Constructs RECAP-based SMILES fragment trees for candidate molecules and experimental fragment trees from low-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (MSn) peak lists, then scores their node-by-node similarity to rank candidate structures de novo using the TeFT method.

## When to use

Use when you need to rank candidate molecule structures predicted from low-resolution MSn spectra (e.g., MS2 peaks from a miniaturized mass spectrometer) by comparing simulated SMILES fragment trees against experimental fragmentation trees.

## When NOT to use

- When spectra are high-resolution and mass accuracy permits direct database search (e.g., SIRIUS/CSI:FingerID) rather than fragment-tree comparison.
- When the unknown molecule falls outside the ten permitted elements (C, H, O, N, P, S, Cl, Br, I, F) or exceeds the 500 Da molecular weight cap.
- When the candidate molecule's SMILES fragment tree exceeds 1500 nodes, as the similarity comparison is unreliable at that scale.

## Inputs

- Low-resolution MSn spectrum peak list (top 5–6 fragmentation peaks with five nearby peak points, limited to max 100 peaks by ion intensity)
- Candidate molecule SMILES fragment trees generated by the TeFT Transformer decoding stage (RECAP-based, ≤1500 nodes, MW <500 Da, elements C/H/O/N/P/S/Cl/Br/I/F only)
- Fragmentation rules from Supplementary Table S1

## Outputs

- Ranked similarity scores for each candidate molecule (e.g., similarity_scores.csv)
- Top-ranked candidate molecule identified as the tested substance
- Annotated fragmentation peaks derived from the highest-scoring SMILES fragment tree

## How to apply

First, parse the RECAP-based SMILES fragment trees of candidate molecules produced by the TeFT Transformer decoding stage, respecting the paper's limits: 1500 nodes total for the SMILES tree, only C, H, O, N, P, S, Cl, Br, I, F elements permitted, and molecular weight <500 Da. Second, build the molecular fragment tree (FT) from the experimental low-resolution MSn peak list using the fragmentation rules in Supplementary Table S1, selecting the top 5–6 fragmentation peaks with their five nearby peak points, capped at 100 peaks by ion intensity. Third, match the two trees node-by-node using the paper's scoring rule table: +6/+3/+0 for matched/partially matched/unmatched parent atoms, +5/+2/+0 for fragment atoms, and +4/+1.5/+0 for neutral losses. Fourth, normalize each raw score by the perfect-match score obtained when both trees are identical. Fifth, rank all candidate molecules by the normalized SMILES fragment tree similarity; the highest-scoring SMILES tree is taken as the tested substance and its tree annotates the fragmentation peaks.

```

```

## Evaluation signals

- The normalized similarity score of the top-ranked candidate matches the expected ranking behavior on the 16 flavonoid alcohols benchmark, where complete structure prediction was achieved for 8 substances.
- All three fragmentation peaks of the Anweiyang capsule m/z 269 marker (formononetin) are fully annotated by the highest-scoring candidate's fragment tree.
- The raw similarity score divided by the perfect-match score (identical trees) yields a value in [0, 1]; scores outside this range indicate a mis-specified normalization.
- The output file (e.g., similarity_scores.csv) contains one row per candidate molecule with its normalized similarity score, matching the count of candidate structures submitted.
- The experimental fragment tree contains no more than 100 peaks and the selected peaks adhere to the top 5–6 fragmentation peaks plus their five nearby points rule.

## Limitations

- TeFT was trained only on positive-ion-mode spectra (H+), restricting its applicability to negative ion mode or other ionization adducts.
- Quantitative comparison with high-resolution alternatives (SIRIUS4, MSNovelist, MassGenie) is not provided; performance on high-resolution spectra may not be exceptional.
- The method requires clear fragmentation rules; ambiguous or non-standard fragmentation pathways may reduce ranking reliability.
